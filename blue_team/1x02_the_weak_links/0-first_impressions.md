# Task 0 — First Impressions Summary

## MedDefense Health Systems Vulnerability Scan

### 1. Scan Metadata

The assessment was performed with **OpenVAS 22.x (Greenbone Community Edition)** against the **10.10.0.0/16 internal address space**, described by the report as covering all internal subnets. The selected policy was **Full and Deep**, with authenticated testing performed wherever credentials were available. The scan was requested by **James Chen, Deputy CISO**, and executed by **SecurePoint Consulting**, a third party. The report records the scan date as **[Current - 5 days]**. It identified **47 hosts responsive during the scan window**.

Authentication depth was not uniform. Linux servers were tested using SSH credentials and Windows systems using domain credentials where available, while **medical devices were scanned unauthenticated because credentials were not provided**. The assessment ran between **02:00 and 06:00** to reduce clinical impact. No active exploitation was attempted; findings were based on version detection, configuration analysis, and authenticated checks. SecurePoint also states an expected OpenVAS false-positive rate of approximately **5–10%** and recommends manual verification before committing remediation resources to high-value findings.

The explicit exclusions are important. The scan **did not cover Microsoft O365 cloud services, physician iPads/mobile devices, or any asset that was offline during the scan window**. This directly leaves **AST-024 Microsoft O365 E3** and **AST-032 Physician iPad fleet** outside the tested population, while assets such as **AST-041 GE Revolution CT scanner** may simply have been absent because they did not respond.

One structural point is also worth noting: the report is not exclusively raw OpenVAS output. **Finding 031 was added manually by SecurePoint following verification of Finding 017**, meaning the dataset contains both automated scanner results and analyst follow-up.

The report's stated totals are **31 findings: 4 Critical, 7 High, 11 Medium, 5 Low, and 4 Informational**.

**Medium is the largest severity category**, representing 11 of 31 findings, or approximately 35.5%. Critical and High findings together also total 11, meaning approximately **35.5% of the report is High or Critical**. The initial picture is therefore broader than the four Critical findings alone: a substantial portion of the dataset consists of configuration, lifecycle, exposure, and defense-in-depth weaknesses that may become important when combined.

A useful caution appears inside the report itself: scanner severity and raw CVSS are not interchangeable. For example, Finding 020 is rated Medium despite showing a CVSS 9.8 because SecurePoint considers the necessary exploitation conditions unlikely and specifically flags it as a possible false positive. Conversely, Finding 003 is scanner-rated Critical even though it is a configuration weakness rather than a CVE. This reinforces the purpose of Task 0: the severity label alone is not sufficient prioritization.

### 4. First Observations

**The four Critical findings are spread across three systems rather than concentrated on one asset.** Findings 001 and 002 are both on **AST-004 billing-srv-01**, Finding 003 is on **AST-002 ehr-db-01 / AST-017 EHR clinical database**, and Finding 004 is on **AST-034 WS-RAD-01 MRI control workstation**. Therefore, half of the Critical count is concentrated on billing-srv-01, but the overall Critical exposure crosses Finance, the clinically critical EHR environment, and diagnostic imaging. From the prior projects, these systems map to **GAP-008** for billing, **GAP-006** for the EHR database, and the imaging environment adjacent to **GAP-001**. They also intersect the **Ransomware Group** threat profile, which explicitly focuses on EHR and known vulnerable systems, and the **Unskilled/Opportunistic Attacker**, which has already been associated with billing compromise.

**Several findings are clearly related rather than independent.** The strongest example is Findings **001 and 002** on **AST-004 billing-srv-01**: the report explicitly states that the remote-code-execution condition in Finding 001 can lead into the local privilege-escalation condition in Finding 002. The same host also has broad MySQL exposure, password-based SSH, unsupported Ubuntu status, and an outdated kernel in Findings 006, 009, 011, and 026. This is more significant as a **systemic billing-host condition** than six isolated scanner entries. That interpretation directly supports **GAP-008**, while the Threat Actor Matrix links the same asset to both organized ransomware and opportunistic exploitation.

**The EHR findings also form a progression.** Finding 017 on **AST-001 ehr-srv-01 / AST-016 EHR application** initially identifies information disclosure and states that the scanner could not confirm whether the AJP connector was active. Finding 031 is then a SecurePoint manual follow-up that confirms the connector. This is a useful example of why scanner output should not be treated as final analysis. The EHR cluster relates directly to **GAP-006**, while **Ransomware Groups** and **Malicious Insiders** both identify the EHR as a particularly significant target in the Threat Actor Matrix.

**Identity exposure is another cluster rather than a single weakness.** **AST-005 ad-dc-01** appears in Findings 007, 018, and 025, involving LDAP configuration, weak Kerberos encryption support, and DNS reconnaissance exposure. This aligns directly with **GAP-007**, which already characterizes Active Directory as only partially protected. The concentration matters particularly for the **Ransomware Group** and **Nation-State APT** threat models because the Threat Actor Matrix identifies AD as a mechanism for broad access, persistence, or ransomware deployment.

**The medical-device results reinforce the pre-existing segmentation problem.** Finding 010 covers **AST-037 BD Alaris infusion pumps**, while Finding 016 covers **AST-036 Philips IntelliVue monitors**; both describe management or network interfaces broadly reachable in the internal environment. That is direct technical evidence supporting **GAP-002 — Medical IoT Is Not Segmented, Monitored, or Recoverable**. It also increases the potential downstream impact of a **Ransomware Group** foothold or a **Negligent Insider**-introduced unmanaged device because the Threat Actor Matrix explicitly identifies the flat internal network as a blast-radius multiplier.

**Recovery infrastructure is itself exposed.** Finding 015 identifies broad access to the management interface of **AST-010 NAS-01**, while Finding 020 concerns **AST-009 backup-srv-01**. This reinforces **GAP-004**, which states that production and recovery copies share the same failure domain. That relationship is particularly important to the **Ransomware Group** threat model because the Threat Actor Matrix explicitly describes attackers identifying and attacking backup infrastructure during enterprise compromise.

**The report also reveals inventory risk, not just vulnerability risk.** Findings 028 and 029 identify two undocumented Linux hosts. Those are already reconciled in the Asset Registry as **AST-013 UNKNOWN-01** and **AST-014 Westside unknown Linux device**, both categorized as Shadow IT. Although neither system is the same asset as **GAP-005's AST-057 Cardiology NAS**, the control theme is similar: unmanaged systems operating on a broadly reachable internal network can become footholds outside normal governance. GAP-005 therefore provides an existing control precedent for how MedDefense treats unmanaged assets. The most relevant threat profiles are the **Unskilled/Opportunistic Attacker**, which exploits exposed vulnerable software, and the **Negligent Insider**, which the matrix links to shadow IT and unmanaged devices.

One result is particularly surprising from a reporting-quality perspective: the headline says **47 responsive hosts**, yet Finding 023 states that approximately **280 workstations** are affected by the USB-control condition, while Finding 027 also relies partly on Sophos Central data. This suggests that at least some findings incorporate domain-policy or asset-management evidence beyond a simple one-to-one set of 47 directly scanned hosts. That is not necessarily wrong, but it means the denominator needs clarification before findings are converted into host-prevalence percentages.

### 5. Scan Limitations

The most important limitation is that this is a **point-in-time network vulnerability assessment, not a complete security assessment**. Only systems responsive during the scan window were counted. The Asset Registry already identifies several documented assets that did not clearly appear in the scan, including **AST-041 GE Revolution CT scanner**, the possible second Westside server, and the physician iPad population. It also notes that some network and physical infrastructure—such as **AST-043 FortiGate 100F, AST-044 Central core switch, AST-045 access-switch fleet, UPS, CCTV, and physical network/server rooms**—does not appear in the scan results.

The scan therefore does **not establish the absence of vulnerabilities on an asset that did not respond**. This matters for the Asset Registry because offline or intermittently connected systems cannot safely be treated as decommissioned. The registry explicitly warns against doing so for the CT scanner, thin clients, mobile devices, and incomplete endpoint populations.

The assessment also provides **less configuration assurance for medical devices** than for credentialed servers because the medical-device portion was unauthenticated. Consequently, the scan provides strong network-level evidence for **GAP-002**—for example, broad interface reachability—but does not represent a full authenticated audit of every device's internal configuration, users, logs, or security settings.

Because **no active exploitation was attempted**, the report does not prove that every scanner-detected condition is practically exploitable in MedDefense's exact environment. SecurePoint explicitly estimates a 5–10% false-positive rate and recommends manual validation. Finding 020 demonstrates this limitation directly. This is why subsequent CVE analysis should validate the technical conditions rather than treating a plugin match as proof of compromise.

The target was the **internal 10.10.0.0/16 network**, so this scan also does not by itself establish what an unauthenticated attacker on the public Internet can reach. For example, **AST-011 web-srv-01** is documented as a DMZ/public-facing system, but its scanned address sits in the Central server addressing range; the Asset Registry specifically states that the actual DMZ and firewall-zone configuration still requires validation. An internal vulnerability scan therefore cannot substitute for an external attack-surface assessment.

Finally, several vulnerability classes are simply **not evidenced as tested by this report**. There is no indication of source-code review, comprehensive web-application business-logic testing, phishing/social-engineering testing, wireless radio-security assessment, physical-security testing, or cloud-configuration review. Those areas should be considered **not demonstrated by this scan**, rather than assumed secure. This distinction is especially relevant to **GAP-003** network-core/physical administrative protection and **GAP-010** cloud/SaaS governance, which depend on evidence outside an OpenVAS internal network scan.

### Overall First Impression

The scan does **not** describe one isolated vulnerability problem. It shows several overlapping exposure themes: a pronounced concentration on **AST-004 billing-srv-01**, clinically important weaknesses around **AST-001/AST-002 EHR**, legacy risk on **AST-034 MRI**, identity weaknesses on **AST-005/AST-006 Active Directory**, broadly reachable medical devices under **AST-036/AST-037**, exposed recovery infrastructure under **AST-009/AST-010**, and two previously undocumented systems.

Those patterns substantially reinforce the earlier gap analysis. In particular, the scan gives technical evidence consistent with **GAP-002, GAP-004, GAP-006, GAP-007, and GAP-008**, while the Threat Actor Matrix shows that the same systems are relevant to **Ransomware Groups, Unskilled/Opportunistic Attackers, Malicious and Negligent Insiders, and—where public services are concerned—Hacktivists**.

The principal Task 0 conclusion is therefore that **severity count alone would be misleading**. The next phase should treat the report as a connected dataset: findings should be validated and prioritized according to the affected asset's role, demonstrated threat pathways, existing control gaps, and whether multiple findings combine into a practical attack chain.
