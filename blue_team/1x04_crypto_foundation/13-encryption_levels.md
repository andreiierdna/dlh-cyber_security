# MedDefense Health Systems
## Task 13 — The Encryption Levels

## 1. Purpose and Evidence Base

This document compares the six encryption levels from Security+ 1.4 and recommends an appropriate protection boundary for each of the seven specified MedDefense data stores. The objective is to protect sensitive information without disrupting EHR access, billing, diagnostic imaging, recovery, or medication delivery.

The recommendations use the following prior work:

- **1x02 — Vulnerability findings:** [Task 21: Vulnerability Assessment](../1x02_the_weak_links/21-vulnerability_assessment.md), particularly Finding 003 (broad PostgreSQL access), Findings 001/002 (billing RCE-to-root chain), Finding 015 (reachable NAS management), Finding 023 (unrestricted USB storage), and Finding 024 (unencrypted DICOM).
- **1x02 — Medical-device findings:** [Task 15: Medical IoT](../1x02_the_weak_links/15-medical_iot.md), including Finding 010, confirmed Alaris default credentials, and the distinction between the incorrect CVE-2020-25165 attribution and later firmware/configuration weaknesses.
- **1x03 — Risk register:** [Task 10: Risk Register](../1x03_defense_blueprint/10-risk_register.md), particularly RISK-002 (EHR disclosure), RISK-005 (exploitation of known weaknesses), RISK-006 (insider information loss), RISK-007 (backup destruction), RISK-008 (medical-device compromise), and RISK-009 (PACS/MRI disruption).
- **1x03 — Security strategy:** [Task 17: Security Strategy](../1x03_defense_blueprint/17-security_strategy.md), especially Sections 6.1, 7.2, and 9.1: approved storage, residual risks, Restricted-data encryption, key lifecycle management, and medical-device exceptions.

They do not establish laptop encryption coverage, the installed MySQL version/edition, or supported on-pump encryption features. The controls below are recommendations, not claims of completed deployment.

## 2. Six-Level Comparison

Performance descriptions are qualitative, not MedDefense benchmark results. Hardware acceleration often makes block encryption inexpensive, but migration, key rotation, indexing changes, and concurrent clinical workloads must be measured separately.

| Level | Scope | Performance Impact | Key Management | MedDefense Use Case | Best Choice — One Sentence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Full-disk** | The entire physical or virtual disk, including operating-system and data areas covered by the implementation; small boot/recovery areas may remain unencrypted. | Usually low ongoing overhead with hardware acceleration; initial encryption and recovery operations add I/O and administrative work. | Typically one disk protection domain with a data-encryption key protected by TPM/startup credentials or a key-encryption key; centrally escrow recovery material per device. | Protect employee laptops against offline access to cached patient, HR, billing, and email data after loss or theft. | Full-disk encryption is the best choice for MedDefense laptops because sensitive copies can appear outside explicitly selected folders. |
| **Partition** | One logical partition on a disk; other partitions remain outside the encryption boundary. | Usually low block-I/O overhead; encrypting only the selected partition reduces the amount of data processed during conversion. | Separate unlock and recovery material for each encrypted partition; document partition layout and protection of swap, temporary files, and other partitions. | A MedDefense server with a verified dedicated data partition could encrypt that partition while leaving an operationally necessary boot partition separate. | Partition encryption is the best choice when a MedDefense server has a fixed, isolated sensitive-data partition and all plaintext spill locations are separately protected. |
| **Volume** | One logical storage volume, which may span partitions or multiple disks. | Usually low block-I/O overhead; throughput depends on CPU capacity, storage latency, and workload concurrency. | Volume data-encryption keys with protected unlock credentials and independent recovery escrow; define startup, expansion, snapshot, and restore procedures. | Encrypt the PostgreSQL, billing, or PACS data volume without changing their database queries or DICOM file formats. | Volume encryption is the best choice for MedDefense server storage that needs transparent protection across a logical storage pool without per-file application changes. |
| **File** | Selected individual files or directories, depending on the implementation. | Variable: per-file processing and key lookup can affect many small files, while bulk backup encryption consumes CPU and I/O during backup/restore. | File or backup-set data keys protected by a master/key-encryption key; preserve versioned recovery material and authorized access when files are copied. | Encrypt backup files before they reach NAS-01 and protect exported Alaris configuration packages in the management repository. | File encryption is the best choice when MedDefense needs backup or configuration artifacts to remain encrypted after copying them to another storage system. |
| **Database** | An entire database or tablespace through database-engine encryption, commonly transparent data encryption (TDE); exact coverage is product-specific. | Often low-to-moderate storage overhead with little query change; initial conversion, rotation, and encrypted backup/restore may add significant work. | Database/tablespace data keys protected by a master key through a supported key-management facility; escrow the keys required for database and backup recovery. | A verified, supported MySQL tablespace-encryption implementation could protect billing database files without changing billing SQL. | Database encryption is the best choice when MedDefense has a supported engine feature that covers the required database artifacts and needs transparent application compatibility. |
| **Record** | Individual fields or records, encrypted by the application or a database function; key placement determines the trust boundary. | Potentially the highest application impact: per-value processing, ciphertext expansion, and loss of ordinary searching, sorting, joins, or indexing on encrypted values. | Fine-grained data keys, key versions, rotation, and application authorization; keep decryption keys outside the database administrator's control when separation is required. | Protect selected EHR identifiers and billing SSNs from database-only access; apply the same fine-grained principle to sensitive O365 messages. | Record encryption is the best choice when MedDefense must keep selected sensitive values unreadable to a database-only account while allowing an authorized application to decrypt them. |

### 2.1 Boundaries That Change the Decision

- **Partition versus volume:** A partition is a defined subdivision of a disk; a logical volume is a storage abstraction that may span disks. For MedDefense, the deciding factor is the actual storage layout and recovery boundary, not a claim that one provides stronger cryptography.
- **Full-disk, partition, and volume encryption:** These protect covered storage when it is locked and the attacker lacks the keys. Once mounted, authorized reads return plaintext. Root on a running MedDefense server can generally read mounted data; these levels do not fix the billing RCE-to-root chain.
- **File encryption:** Protection can follow an artifact only if the copied object remains ciphertext. NAS shared-folder encryption alone returns plaintext to an authorized mounted-share reader; backup-application encryption before upload gives NAS-01 a different boundary.
- **Database encryption:** TDE protects covered database files, not ordinary SQL results. A legitimate database account still receives plaintext for permitted queries. Data files, transaction logs, temporary storage, exports, and backups require explicit coverage checks; encrypting a tablespace does not prove all are protected.
- **Record encryption:** A database function using keys available to the same database administrator does not provide meaningful separation from that administrator. For MedDefense's EHR and billing confidentiality requirement, use application-side encryption with separately controlled keys. An authorized application, compromised application identity, or clinician permitted to view the value can still obtain plaintext.

**Clinical implication:** Transparent storage encryption minimizes workflow changes, whereas encrypting every EHR field individually could break patient lookup and clinical reporting. Use broad storage encryption as the baseline and add record encryption only where its access separation justifies the application changes.

### 3.1 Database Decision: Why Not Simply Enable TDE?

**PostgreSQL:** The audit specifies PostgreSQL 14; community PostgreSQL 14 does not provide built-in TDE. Recommend volume encryption rather than presenting an unavailable native setting as a remediation. Record encryption requires an EHR application change and a supported key-management integration, not merely a storage switch.

**MySQL:** The installed version, edition, storage engines, and encryption/key-management features are not documented. Volume encryption is therefore the baseline recommendation. Database-level encryption is a valid alternative only after verifying supported tablespace encryption, durable key recovery, and coverage of required logs, temporary storage, and backups; it does not remove the need for record-level separation where that is required.

**Legitimate-query example:** A permitted `SELECT` of a patient identifier returns plaintext under volume encryption or TDE. With application-side record encryption and keys unavailable to the SQL user, the same query returns ciphertext; the authorized EHR application decrypts it for the clinician. A clinician authorized to view the record can still copy the displayed value, so RISK-006 remains relevant.

### 3.2 NAS Decision: Confidentiality Is Not Recoverability

NAS-01's documented Synology shared-folder encryption is optional defense in depth, not the primary backup-encryption boundary. Once the folder is mounted, an authorized NAS reader can obtain its contents; encrypting backup sets before upload leaves those contents encrypted even in that state.

Keep protected, versioned recovery keys and tested recovery instructions outside NAS-01. A separately administered escrow copy must remain available if production identity or the NAS is lost. Retain the strategy's immutable offsite copies and restricted backup administration: encryption cannot stop an attacker from deleting encrypted backups or encrypting them again.

### 3.3 O365 Decision: Message Protection Is Not a Blanket Access Guarantee

Message-level controls must define who can decrypt a PHI message and which forwarding/access restrictions the selected policy supports. Purview Message Encryption is not equivalent to S/MIME end-to-end encryption with solely recipient-controlled private keys, and it does not prevent an authorized recipient from recording displayed content. Use S/MIME only if MedDefense explicitly requires that different trust model and can manage physician certificates and recipient interoperability.

Test actual physician and external-recipient workflows before enforcement. Provider storage encryption remains in place, but does not by itself authorize emailing PHI or protect data copied to an unencrypted local device; laptop full-disk encryption and the approved-handling policy cover those separate boundaries.

### 3.4 Alaris Decision: Integrity and Safety Come First

The prior assessment rejects the specific CVE-2020-25165 attribution to detected software 12.1.2 while retaining the genuine default-credential and isolation finding. It also identifies CVE-2023-30559 (improperly signed wireless firmware) and CVE-2023-30560 (configuration lacking authentication) in the relevant device generation. These are integrity/authentication problems, not evidence that hospital-installed disk encryption is appropriate.

For stored exports, use file encryption to protect confidential configuration and credentials. For packages applied to a pump, require the supported vendor authenticity checks; encryption is not a substitute for a valid digital signature. BD retains firmware-signing private keys; MedDefense manages repository encryption keys and authorized maintenance access.

If BD cannot confirm supported on-device encryption, document that limitation as a medical-device cryptographic exception with the Biomedical Engineering Lead/Clinical Operations risk owner. Apply approved credential changes, targeted segmentation, restricted physical/maintenance access, and vendor-supported remediation. Do not claim the exception eliminates the residual RISK-008 exposure.

## 4. Key Management and Operational Acceptance

The [Security Strategy, Section 9.1](../1x03_defense_blueprint/17-security_strategy.md#91-connection-to-project-1x04--cryptographic-foundation) requires key ownership, generation, storage, escrow, rotation, and destruction. Each recommended boundary must therefore have a named custodian and recovery procedure before production cutover.

| Store / Custodian | Key and Access Requirement | Acceptance Evidence Required Before Deployment Is Considered Complete |
|---|---|---|
| **EHR — Sarah Park / EHR application owner** | Protect volume unlock material; escrow recovery independently. Restrict record-decryption permissions to approved application identities, not database-only administrators. | An offline storage copy is unreadable without keys; representative patient lookup, write, clinical reporting, restart, and restore work within agreed clinical limits; a database-only query cannot decrypt selected protected fields. |
| **NAS-01 — Sarah Park / backup administrator** | Separate backup encryption keys from NAS access and ordinary production administration; retain keys needed for every retained backup version. | Restore an encrypted EHR or billing backup into an isolated environment after simulating loss of NAS-local keys and ordinary production credentials; verify immutable-copy protection and measured RTO/RPO. |
| **Billing — Sarah Park / billing application owner** | Separate volume recovery material and field-encryption authorization; document the shared-host root limitation. | Authorized billing lookup, claims processing, reporting, rotation, and restore preserve correct results; database-only access does not reveal selected encrypted identifiers; host remediation is verified separately. |
| **PACS — Radiology / IT** | Escrow volume recovery keys and define unlock/restart procedures compatible with imaging operations. | Ingest, retrieve, and display representative images and patient headers correctly; measure retrieval latency under representative load and prove repository/metadata recovery. |
| **O365 — IT / messaging administrator** | Retain provider-managed storage keys; govern message-decryption rights and tenant policies, with certificate lifecycle management if S/MIME is chosen. | Intended internal and external recipients can open protected messages/attachments; an unintended identity is denied; policy behavior and user workflow are documented. |
| **Laptops — IT / endpoint administrator** | Use supported TPM-based protection and centrally escrow recovery material with restricted, audited access; document protection for every fixed data volume. | Verify encryption coverage, offline-read protection, normal startup, and authorized recovery on a representative managed laptop. |
| **Alaris — Biomedical Engineering / BD** | Protect exported artifact keys in MedDefense's approved key-management boundary; leave firmware-signing authority with BD. | Confirm actual supported controls and package authenticity checks; demonstrate configuration recovery and clinical/maintenance workflow safety on a non-patient-care device, with Biomedical/Clinical sign-off. |

These are **required future acceptance checks**, not reported test results. Initial encryption, rotation, storage relocation, and recovery must use approved maintenance windows and rollback procedures; no unsupported pump modification or bulk clinical cutover is authorized by this document.

Encryption at rest also does not repair the audit's plaintext PostgreSQL/MySQL connections or Finding 024's DICOM transport. Enforce approved encrypted transport independently, with vendor-approved compensating controls where the legacy MRI cannot support the target baseline.

## 5. Recommendation

Use **volume encryption for EHR, billing, and PACS**, **file/backup-set encryption for NAS-01 and exported Alaris artifacts**, **message-level protection for sensitive O365 email**, and **full-disk encryption for employee laptops**. Add **record encryption** to selected EHR and billing values only where database-access separation is required and application workflows can support it.

Partition encryption and database-level encryption remain valid options when verified storage layouts or engine capabilities make them appropriate; MedDefense should not assign every level merely to fill the taxonomy. The existing strategy fully allocates its $120,000 Year-1 portfolio, so new application changes, key-management services, licenses, or migration work require explicit costing and funding rather than an assumption that encryption is already included. Keep the prior segmentation, patching, immutable recovery, and clinical-safety requirements: encrypted storage alone does not resolve the registered MedDefense risks.
